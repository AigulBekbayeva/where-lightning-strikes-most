Where Lightning Strikes Most

Where on Earth do thunderstorms happen most often? How is lightning linked to humidity, terrain and tropical climate zones?

Data: NASA LIS/OTD, ERA5

The thunderstorm belt

The LIS and OTD sensors counted flashes from space for 20 years. Earth produces about 44 flashes per second on average, and most of them happen in the tropics.

Land heats up faster than the ocean, which drives stronger updrafts. In those clouds, ice crystals and graupel collide more often — and those collisions are what separate electric charge. The air over the ocean is warm and moist too, but it rises more weakly, so storms there are much rarer.

Moisture is needed, but not enough

Choose what to compare lightning with. Over the ocean, even the most humid air produces few flashes: storms also need an unstable atmosphere (CAPE) and something to lift the air — heated land, mountains, converging breezes.

Climate zones

Köppen zones are computed from ERA5 temperature and precipitation over the same period. As expected, the tropics lead. Compare the tropical rainforest (Af), monsoon (Am) and savanna (Aw) climates with each other: they differ in the length of the dry season.

Terrain and the lightning capital

The world record holder is the southern part of Lake Maracaibo in Venezuela, with about 232 flashes per km² per year. The warm lake is boxed in by Andean ranges; at night, mountain–valley and lake breezes converge over the water and set off storms nearly 300 nights a year. Second place goes to the mountains near Kabare in the eastern DR Congo. Of the 500 hotspots, 283 are in Africa, and the strongest are almost always tied to complex terrain.

    The hotspots were computed from LIS on a 0.1° grid (1998–2013), while the map layer uses a 0.5° grid. That is why map cells show lower values: a larger cell averages the peak with its surroundings.
